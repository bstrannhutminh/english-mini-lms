# Urban Green Spaces and Public Health

More than half of the world's population now lives in cities, and by 2050 the United Nations expects that figure to approach seven in ten. This great migration to urban life has brought prosperity and opportunity, but it has also crowded millions of people into environments of concrete, glass, and traffic, often far from nature. Parks, gardens, street trees, and riverside paths — collectively known as urban green spaces — have long been valued as places of beauty and recreation. Increasingly, however, researchers and policymakers regard them as essential infrastructure for public health: living systems that clean the air, cool the streets, and offer residents somewhere to move, meet, and recover from the stresses of city life.

The physical health benefits of green space are now well documented. People who live near parks are more likely to walk, run, and cycle, and studies across Europe and North America have linked proximity to greenery with lower rates of obesity, heart disease, and type 2 diabetes. Trees also improve air quality by filtering particulates and absorbing pollutants, while vegetation helps to counter the "urban heat island" effect, in which built-up areas can be several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside. During heatwaves — which are becoming more frequent as the climate warms — shaded parks can literally save lives, offering cool refuge to older residents and young children, the groups most vulnerable to extreme heat. Doctors in several countries have even begun issuing "green prescriptions", advising patients to spend regular time in parks as part of treatment for conditions ranging from high blood pressure to mild depression.

The effects on mental health may be equally important. A growing body of research shows that even brief exposure to nature lowers blood pressure, reduces levels of the stress hormone cortisol, and improves mood and concentration. Psychologists explain this through "attention restoration theory": natural environments engage the mind gently, allowing the focused attention exhausted by screens, traffic, and deadlines to recover. During the COVID-19 lockdowns, when indoor life became the norm, parks became a lifeline for millions, and surveys recorded sharp rises in anxiety among those without access to green space. Regular contact with nature has also been associated with lower rates of depression and with faster recovery from mental fatigue.

Green spaces also strengthen the social fabric of neighbourhoods. A well-used park is a meeting place where neighbours encounter one another, children play together, and community events take root. Research in several cities has found that residents of greener streets report stronger social ties and a greater sense of safety, and some studies have even linked the greening of vacant lots with reductions in crime. Community gardens go further still, giving residents — including older people and recent immigrants — a shared purpose, fresh produce, and a reason to spend time outdoors. In an age when loneliness is increasingly recognised as a public health problem in its own right, these informal social benefits deserve to be taken seriously. For children, unstructured play in green settings supports motor development, creativity, and risk assessment in ways that indoor environments rarely match.

Yet access to green space is strikingly unequal. In city after city, wealthy districts enjoy leafy avenues and well-maintained parks, while poorer neighbourhoods make do with sparse, neglected plots — or none at all. This "green gap" mirrors wider health inequalities: the communities that would benefit most from parks are often those least likely to have them. Efforts to close the gap can also backfire. When a neglected area gains an attractive new park, property values often rise, and long-standing residents may be priced out — a process researchers call "green gentrification". Planners therefore face a delicate task: improving green infrastructure without displacing the very people it is meant to serve, for example by pairing park investments with affordable housing protections.

Despite these challenges, the direction of travel is encouraging. Cities from Singapore to Paris are weaving nature into their plans — planting street trees, converting disused railways into green corridors, and requiring new developments to include gardens and rooftop planting. The "15-minute city" concept, which aims to put parks, schools, and shops within a short walk of every home, has gained ground since the pandemic. Urban forests also support biodiversity, providing habitat for birds, pollinators, and other wildlife that would otherwise vanish from city life. Economists note that such investments often pay for themselves: every dollar spent on urban greenery returns several dollars in reduced healthcare costs, higher productivity, and increased property tax revenue. As cities continue to grow, the question is no longer whether we can afford green spaces, but whether we can afford to be without them. For public health officials, the message is clear: a park is not a luxury, but a prescription.
